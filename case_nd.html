Personal / Family Project · UX/UI Design

Project ND

An experimental AI-assisted learning app that helps students reason through homework instead of just finishing it, built around my younger brother's needs.

Role Designer · Builder Timeline Sept 24, 2026 – Present Stack Claude, Gemini Skills Interaction Design · AI-Assisted Development · Prototyping Status Paused by choice

01 — Why I Built This

ND is an experimental AI-assisted learning application designed to support qualitative reasoning, sentence formation, and independent learning for neurodivergent students. Initially I designed it as a homework assistance tool. However, the project evolved into an exploration of how structured guidance and adaptive questioning could help students develop a deeper understanding of their assignments rather than simply completing them. The application aims to break down actual homework assignments and AI-generated practice questions into manageable steps. Through simplified language, progressive hints, and strategic redirection, it encourages users to formulate their own responses and strengthen their reasoning skills.

The prototype was developed around my younger brother's specific learning needs, making the project both a personal exploration of accessible education and an exercise in designing for individual cognitive differences.

The Challenge

When my younger brother struggles with homework, asking for assistance often leads to one of two outcomes: receiving the answer directly or becoming dependent on prolonged guidance. Neither approach consistently supports independent understanding and future ability. Instead, homework becomes an exercise in completing assignments for a grade rather than retaining and applying the underlying concepts. This creates frustration for both the student and the parent, particularly when daily assignments require extensive one-on-one guidance. The challenge was to explore how newer technology could provide meaningful assistance without replacing the cognitive effort necessary for learning.

The Goal

Design an interactive learning tool that helps students work through assignments independently by breaking complex tasks into smaller, approachable steps. Rather than supplying answers, the application should encourage reasoning through adaptive prompts, progressive hints, and constructive redirection. The intended outcomes were to strengthen independent reasoning and sentence formation, reduce cognitive overload during homework, and lessen reliance on continuous parental supervision.

02 — How It Works

Homework goes in as pasted text. From there, the app's whole job is pacing and framing — one question, restated plainly, with help that escalates only as needed.

Step 01 — Paste It In

The full assignment goes into a prompt area, no formatting cleanup required.

Step 02 — One at a Time

The app breaks the set apart and surfaces a single question, restated in plain language.

Step 03 — A Hint If He's Stuck

Hints only show up if he asks. Each tier gives away a little more — where to look, then narrowing it down, then walking the first part alongside him — without just handing over the answer.

Step 04 — His Part

He answers in his own words. The app tracks whether both parts of the question got covered, not whether the wording matches anything exactly.

03 — Ideation

Phase 01 — Homework Assistance

The initial concept focused on a web application that could organize pasted assignments into individual questions, allowing students to complete their homework one task at a time.

Phase 02 — Reasoning Through Homework

As the concept developed, I began questioning whether breaking down homework alone would meaningfully support learning. The focus shifted toward encouraging students to explain their reasoning, interpret questions, and construct their own responses. Rather than correcting mistakes by immediately revealing answers, the tool would redirect students toward the underlying concepts.

Phase 03 — Cognitive Scaffolding & Language Formation

The final direction expanded beyond homework completion to explore how adaptive scaffolding could support everyday language formulation and working memory during moments of cognitive overload. This introduced a broader design question: how might an AI-assisted learning environment provide individualized cognitive support without replacing the student's independent thought process?

04 — Design & Prototyping

I began prototyping directly through Claude, using AI-assisted development to translate early concepts into an interactive web experience. Rather than following a traditional design-to-development workflow, I used iterative prompting to explore interface structures, learning interactions, and the technical feasibility of different features.

Design Principles

  • Progressive disclosure: presenting one question at a time to reduce overwhelming amounts of information.
  • Language simplification: restating complex instructions in more approachable language without changing their intended meaning.
  • Scaffolded reasoning: providing incremental hints that encourage students to construct answers independently.
  • Constructive redirection: guiding students to reconsider their reasoning rather than immediately identifying an answer as incorrect.
  • Contextual learning: incorporating subject-specific materials and actual homework to make interactions relevant to the student's coursework.

Throughout development, I experimented with incorporating materials from different school subjects to explore how the application could adapt its guidance to different learning contexts.

  • English / ELA

Plain Language, Original Kept

Every question appears twice: as written on the worksheet, and restated in plain words. The plain version is always shown and never costs a hint. The trade-off is that a restatement can drift from what the teacher meant, so the original wording stays on screen beside it.

Hints on Request, in Four Tiers

Help only appears when he asks, and it escalates: where to look, narrowing it down, walking through it, then the answer with an explanation. The default is effort rather than rescue. The trade-off is that a stuck student may not ask, so even the final tier is framed as a normal place to stop.

A Chat-Style Thread

One question at a time, in a conversational thread that can respond to his actual wording. The trade-off is that chat invites open-ended back-and-forth, which is the dependency the project set out to avoid, so each reply points back to the part of the question still open.

Coverage Over Wording

His answer is checked for whether each part of the question was addressed, not whether it matches an expected phrasing. The trade-off is that judging coverage takes real interpretation, and this is the behavior I could not properly evaluate.

05 — Going Forward

Working on ND helped me recognize the distinction between creating a functional AI-powered interface and designing a learning experience that genuinely supports cognitive development. Although AI-assisted development allowed me to prototype ideas beyond my current programming experience, I encountered limitations in my ability to implement, evaluate, and refine the adaptive learning behaviors I originally envisioned.

More importantly, the project raised questions about how to determine whether an AI system is actually improving comprehension rather than simply making assignments easier to complete. For these reasons, I decided to place ND on hiatus rather than continue expanding a prototype without the technical foundation or evaluation methods necessary to support its intended purpose. I hope to revisit the project as I develop stronger skills in programming, UX research, and AI interaction design, with a greater emphasis on evaluating learning outcomes through real user testing.

What's Next

  • Investigating the cognitive processing of multi-step academic tasks in neurodivergent learners.
  • Validating whether the "hint ladder" pacing aligns with natural problem-solving behaviors.
  • Translating research insights into dynamic question restructuring, moving beyond simply adjusting hint frequency.

Key Takeaway

Designing for accessibility is not simply about reducing the difficulty of a task. It is about providing the right level of support so that users can develop the confidence and ability to complete it independently.
